Water particles pull on each other

MAT-073 · Cohesion — Explain why water forms rounded droplets by referring to the attraction between water particles.

Script
SayScreen
Suppose you drip water onto a penny, one drop at a time.[a dropper over a penny seen from the side; drops land]
The water does not run off; it piles into a rounded dome.[the dome rises and rounds with each drop]
The penny holds 25 drops before the dome spills. What holds the water together?[a counter reaches 25; question mark over the dome]
The water's own particles hold it together.[the dome; chip: ITS OWN PARTICLES]
Water is made of tiny particles, packed close and touching.[zoom into the dome: a heap of touching gray circles]
Every water particle pulls on the particles next to it.[double-headed arrows appear between neighboring circles]
This double-headed arrow means the two particles pull on each other, not that they move.[the key: two circles, the arrow, the words a pull, not a movement]
An outer particle has neighbors only on its inner side, so it is pulled in toward the rest.[arrows on the outer circles, each pointing inward]
Water pulling on water is one substance pulling on itself.[label: water on water]
The pull between particles of the same substance is called cohesion.[chip: COHESION]
A raindrop on a leaf sits as a rounded bead, not a flat sheet.[a bead on a leaf beside a dashed flat sheet, crossed out]
When two drops touch, they join into one bigger drop.[before and after: two drops, then one]
So, here's what we've seen. Water particles pull on each other.[the rule slide: the dome zoom with its inward arrows; chip COHESION; nothing else printed]
Cohesion pulls the outer particles of a drop in toward the rest, so the water gathers into a rounded shape.[the heap rounds into a dome]
So on the penny, cohesion pulls the outer particles in, and the water stands as a dome.[the penny and its dome]
Okay, now your turn.[closing screen: one line — “Water particles pull on each other.”]

“Okay, now your turn.” — the attached question

video

  1. A It pulls it together into a dome.
  2. B It pushes it off the edge.
  3. C It turns it into water vapor.
Answer

Key: A — It pulls it together into a dome.

Water particles pull on each other: cohesion.
Cohesion pulls the water's outer particles in toward the rest.
So the water on the penny stands as a rounded dome.

Feedback per option

  • A Cohesion pulls the outer particles in toward the rest, so the water stands as a dome.
  • B Nothing pushes the water off the edge; cohesion pulls the water together.
  • C The water stays liquid; cohesion pulls it together into a dome.

Water pulls on other surfaces too

MAT-074 · Adhesion — Distinguish cohesion from adhesion by identifying which involves attraction between the same substance and which involves different substances.

Script
SayScreen
Suppose you dip the corner of a paper towel into a glass of water.[a glass; a towel strip dips in]
The water climbs up the towel, above the water in the glass.[the wet part rises above the level]
When you tip the glass and pour the water out, a thin film stays stuck inside.[the glass tips, empties, and stands again with a film on its inside]
Cohesion pulls water toward water; what pulls water toward paper and glass?[question mark between the towel and the glass]
The paper's particles and the glass's particles do.[zoom on the towel: gray circles beside a hatched band labeled paper; pull arrows to the band]
That pull drags the water's edge up the towel.[the edge climbs]
In the glass, water pulls on glass, so a film stays stuck.[gray circles pulled to a hatched band labeled glass]
Water pulling on paper or glass is two different substances pulling on each other.[label: water on paper; water on glass]
The pull between particles of different substances is called adhesion.[chip: ADHESION]
Cohesion is water pulling on water; adhesion is water pulling on something else.[the two-panel figure: water on water, water on glass]
To sort a case, ask what is pulling on what.[chip: WHAT PULLS ON WHAT?]
A drop beading on a leaf is water on water, so it is cohesion; water climbing a towel is water on paper, so it is adhesion.[tick over the bead; cross over the towel; chip above: SAME SUBSTANCE?]
So, here's what we've seen. Water particles also pull on the particles of other substances, such as glass and paper.[the rule slide: the five cases under the question; chips COHESION, ADHESION; nothing else printed]
The pull between particles of different substances is called adhesion.[chip ADHESION brightens]
Okay, now your turn.[closing screen: one line — “Water particles also pull on the particles of other substances, such as glass and paper.”]

The surface acts like a stretched skin

MAT-075 · Surface tension — Explain how surface tension allows small objects to rest on water without sinking.

Script
SayScreen
Suppose you drop a steel paper clip into water: it sinks.[the clip falls to the bottom]
Steel has a density of about 8 grams per cubic centimeter, and water 1.0 gram per cubic centimeter, so steel is denser.[the two-row density table]
Now suppose you lay a dry clip gently on the water: it rests, and the surface dips under it.[the clip lowered; the surface line dips under it]
Why does the same clip sink one time and rest the next?[question mark between the two glasses]
The water's surface holds it up.[the resting clip; the dipped surface highlighted]
Steel is denser than water, so the resting clip is not floating.[label: not floating]
Water particles pull on each other; that pull is cohesion.[zoom: gray circles with pull arrows]
A particle inside has neighbors all around, so it is pulled from every side.[particle P ringed; six arrows]
A particle at the surface has no water above it, so its neighbors pull it only sideways and down.[particle S in the top row; four arrows, none upward]
So the surface particles hold together like a tight, stretched skin.[arrows along the whole top row]
This skin effect is called surface tension.[chip: SURFACE TENSION]
Laid on gently, the clip dips the skin without breaking it, so it rests.[the sagging top row under the clip]
Dropped hard, the clip punches through the skin, and steel, denser than water, sinks.[the clip breaks the row and falls]
So, here's what we've seen. The surface particles hold together like a tight, stretched skin.[the rule slide: the skin zoom, the top row with its sideways-and-down arrows; chip SURFACE TENSION; nothing else printed]
This skin effect is called surface tension.[chip SURFACE TENSION brightens]
Okay, now your turn.[closing screen: one line — “The surface particles hold together like a tight, stretched skin.”]

  2. B the water at the surface is thicker than the water below
  3. C the surface skin of the water holds its feet up
Answer

Key: C — the surface skin of the water holds its feet up

The surface particles hold together like a stretched skin: surface tension.
The mosquito's feet press the skin down a little but do not break it.
So the skin holds the mosquito up.

Feedback per option

  • A Floating is for things less dense than water; the mosquito stands on the surface skin, denting it but not breaking it.
  • B The surface water is the same water; the skin comes from the surface particles pulling on each other.
  • C The mosquito's feet dent the surface skin but do not break it, so the skin holds the mosquito up.
Q11transfer

A sewing needle rests on water. A fingertip pushes it down through the surface. What happens next?

  1. A it rises back to the surface
  2. B it floats just under the surface
  3. C it sinks to the bottom
Answer

Key: C — it sinks to the bottom

The surface skin held the needle up while it was unbroken.
The fingertip pushes the needle through the skin.
Below the skin, steel is denser than water, so the needle sinks to the bottom.

Feedback per option

  • A The skin held the needle up only while it was unbroken; steel denser than water does not rise.
  • B Steel is denser than water, so below the skin the needle sinks all the way down.
  • C The skin is broken, and steel is denser than water, so the needle sinks.
Q12what

Is a steel clip resting on water floating the same way a cork floats?

  1. A yes
  2. B no
Answer

Key: B — no

A cork is less dense than water, so it floats.
Steel is denser than water, so a steel clip cannot float.
The resting clip is held up by the surface skin, so it is not floating the way a cork floats.

Feedback per option

  • A A cork floats because it is less dense than water. Steel is denser than water, so the clip is held up by the surface skin, not floating.
  • B Steel is denser than water, so the clip cannot float like a cork. The surface skin holds the clip up.
