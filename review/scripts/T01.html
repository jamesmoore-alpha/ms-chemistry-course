What counts as matter

MAT-001 · What matter is — Decide whether something is matter or not by checking whether it has mass and takes up space.

Script
SayScreen
Here is a flat soccer ball on a balance.[flat ball on a balance; readout 412 g]
The balance reads 412 grams.[readout pulses]
A student pumps the ball up hard and puts it back.[pump; ball rounds out; back on the pan]
Now the balance reads 417 grams.[readout changes to 417 g]
The balance measures how much material is in the ball, and scientists call that amount its mass.[chip: MASS]
Only air went in, so the air has mass, about 5 grams of it.[label AIR on the ball; chip: HAS MASS]
And the pumped ball is bigger, so the air takes up space.[chip: TAKES UP SPACE]
Anything that has mass and takes up space is called matter.[chip: MATTER]
Air has mass and takes up space, so air is matter.[tick beside MATTER]
Here are some more examples.[four empty panels appear]
A stone has mass and takes up space, so a stone is matter.[stone panel; tick]
Water has mass and takes up space, so water is matter.[water panel; tick]
But now consider the light from a lamp.[lamp shining on the empty pan; readout unchanged]
Light has no mass and takes up no space, so light is not matter.[lamp panel; cross]
And a shadow has no mass and takes up no space, so a shadow is not matter.[shadow panel; cross]
So, here's what we've seen. Matter is anything that has mass and takes up space.[the rule slide: the flat ball and the pumped ball; chips HAS MASS, TAKES UP SPACE, MATTER; nothing else printed]
So the extra 5 grams on the balance was air, and air is matter.[417 g readout]
Okay, now your turn.[the rule slide's images hold; closing screen: one line — “Matter is anything that has mass and takes up space.”]

Matter is made of particles too small to see

MAT-002 · Matter is made of particles too small to see — State that all matter is made up of particles that cannot be seen.

Script
SayScreen

A student tips in 10 grams of sugar and stirs.[sugar grains fall in; spoon stirs]
The grains shrink, and vanish.[grains shrink to nothing]
The water looks clear.[clear glass]
She sips the water, and the water tastes sweet.[sip; chip: TASTES SWEET]
So the sugar is still there.[chip: SUGAR STILL HERE]
Let's zoom in.[magnifier circle grows over the water]
Where did the sugar go?[question mark on the glass]
It broke into pieces far too small to see.[sugar particles appear among water particles]
And those pieces spread all through the water.[sugar particles drift apart through the water particles]
Scientists call these tiny pieces particles.[chip: PARTICLES]
One particle is far too small to see, and far too small to taste.[one sugar particle isolated, then shrinks to a dot]
A grain of sugar you can see is billions of particles packed together.[dot multiplies into a grain]
The water is made of particles too.[water particles, labelled WATER PARTICLES]
And it is not only sugar and water.[zoom out]
A stone is made of particles.[stone; magnifier shows particles]
The air in a soccer ball is made of particles.[ball; magnifier shows particles far apart]
So, here's what we've seen. All matter is made of particles that are far too small to see.[the rule slide: the glass, the stone and the ball, each with its magnifier of particles; chip PARTICLES; nothing else printed]
The sugar is still in the glass, as billions of particles too small to see.[clear glass with magnifier]
Okay, now your turn.[the rule slide's images hold; closing screen: one line — “All matter is made of particles that are far too small to see.”]

Particles never stop moving

MAT-003 · Particles are always moving — Explain an observation (such as a smell spreading across a room) using the idea that particles are constantly in motion.

Script
SayScreen
Here are students in a quiet classroom, seen from above. The windows are shut, and no fan is running.[room plan; students at desks; back row labelled; chips: WINDOWS SHUT, NO FAN]
The teacher opens a bottle of perfume at the front.[bottle opens; a few dark particles appear at its neck]
Twenty seconds later, the back row can smell it, and nobody carried the smell to them.[timer runs 0 to 20 s; back-row desks highlight]
So how did the perfume cross the room?[question mark]
Its particles moved across by themselves.[perfume particles drift across the plan, faintly]
Perfume is matter, so perfume is made of particles.[magnifier on the bottle neck: perfume particles]
Some perfume particles leave the open bottle.[perfume particles drift out]
Particles are always moving.[chip: ALWAYS MOVING; circles jiggle and travel]
The air is made of particles too, and they are moving.[air particles fill the room, all moving]
The perfume particles bump into air particles.[a perfume particle hits a air particle]
They bounce off and keep moving, in every direction.[perfume particles scatter in all directions]
So the perfume particles spread through the whole room.[perfume particles spread across the plan]
After twenty seconds, some reach the back row.[perfume particles at the back-row desks; timer at 20 s; chip: SMELL REACHES THE BACK ROW]
Now consider a glass of still water, with one drop of food coloring and no stirring.[glass; drop falls in]
By the next morning the whole glass is colored.[color spreads slowly through the glass; clock advances]
Particles never stop moving, so the coloring particles moved through the whole glass.[magnifier: perfume particles moving among air particles]
Particles are always moving.[the rule slide: the room plan and the glass; chip ALWAYS MOVING; nothing else printed]
They never stop.[the rule slide holds]
So in the classroom, moving perfume particles spread from the bottle to the back row.[room plan at 20 s]
That is how the smell crossed a still room.[back row highlighted]
Okay, now your turn.[the rule slide's images hold; closing screen: one line — “Particles are always moving. They never stop.”]

Heating speeds particles up; cooling slows them down

MAT-004 · Heating speeds particles up; cooling slows them down — Predict how changing the temperature of a substance affects the speed of its particles.

Script
SayScreen
Here are two mugs, each with 250 milliliters of water.[two mugs; labels 250 mL]
This one is at 10 degrees Celsius, and this one is at 80 degrees Celsius.[labels 10 °C and 80 °C appear on the words]
A student drops a tea bag into each, and does not stir.[two tea bags drop in]
After thirty seconds, the hot water is brown all the way through.[timer runs; hot mug fully brown]
The cold water shows only a faint cloud around the bag.[cold mug: faint cloud]
The tea and the water are the same in both mugs, so what is different about the hot water?[question mark between the mugs]
Its particles move faster.[chip on the hot mug: FASTER PARTICLES]
Let's zoom into the water.[two particle boxes appear below the mugs]
Water is made of particles, and particles are always moving.[particles jiggle in both boxes]
Heating a substance makes its particles move faster.[80 °C box: its particles slide visibly faster, no arrows; chip: HIGHER TEMPERATURE, FASTER PARTICLES]
Cooling makes them move more slowly.[10 °C box: its particles slide visibly slower, no arrows]
Tea particles leave the bag and bump into the moving water particles.[dark tea particles enter each box]
Faster water particles knock the tea particles around faster.[dark particles scatter quickly in the hot box, slowly in the cold]
So the brown color spreads through the hot mug sooner.[hot mug brown; chip: COLOR SPREADS SOONER]
The particles do not grow or shrink; only their speed changes.[both boxes: particles same size; chip: ONLY THE SPEED CHANGES]
So, heating makes particles move faster. Cooling makes them move more slowly.[the rule slide: the two particle boxes, the 80 °C particles moving fast and the 10 °C particles slowly, no arrows; chips HEATING: FASTER, COOLING: SLOWER; nothing else printed]
So in the two mugs, the tea and the water were the same.[two mugs]
Heating only made the hot water's particles move faster, so the color spread sooner.[hot mug brown]
Okay, now your turn.[the rule slide's images hold; closing screen: one line — “Heating makes particles move faster; cooling makes them move more slowly.”]

Is it a solid, a liquid or a gas?

MAT-016 · Solid, liquid or gas — tell them apart — Classify a sample as a solid, a liquid or a gas from whether it keeps its own shape and whether it keeps its own volume.

Script
SayScreen
Here is a student pouring dry sand into a jar. It takes the jar's shape, just as water would.[a student pours sand; it piles to the jar's shape; water pours into a second jar]
She says sand is a liquid, because it pours.[the student beside the two jars]
We ask two questions: does it keep its own shape, and does it keep its own volume?[chips: KEEPS ITS SHAPE? KEEPS ITS VOLUME?]
A steel spoon keeps its shape and volume in a cup, a bowl and a pitcher, so it is a solid.[spoon moves container to container, unchanged; two ticks; label SOLID]
Here is 250 milliliters of juice.[juice in a tall glass; label 250 mL]
In the glass it stands tall; in a wide bowl it lies flat.[juice pours glass to bowl]
A measuring cup still reads 250 milliliters.[measuring cup reads 250 mL]
It takes its container's shape but keeps its volume, so the juice is a liquid.[cross on shape, tick on volume; label LIQUID]
Here is 20 milliliters of air sealed in a syringe.[syringe at 20 mL]
She pulls the plunger out, and the air fills 60 milliliters.[plunger out to 60 mL]
She pushes it in, and the same air fits into 10 milliliters.[plunger in to 10 mL]
It keeps neither shape nor volume, so air is a gas.[two crosses; label GAS]
Honey flows slowly, but it still takes its jar's shape and keeps its volume. So honey is a liquid.[honey pours; cross, tick; label LIQUID]
Back at the sand, one grain keeps its own shape and volume, so it is a solid.[the jar of sand; zoom to a single grain; two ticks; label SOLID]
A jar of sand is thousands of tiny solids.[zoom out to the jar]
So, here's what we've seen.[the rule slide: the spoon, the juice and the syringe; nothing else printed]
A solid keeps its own shape and its own volume.[the spoon; two ticks]
A liquid keeps its own volume but takes the shape of its container.[the juice; cross, tick]
A gas keeps neither: it spreads to fill whatever space it is given.[the syringe; two crosses]
So, when she said sand is a liquid, she was not right. Sand is a solid, in very small pieces.[the jar of sand; label SOLID]
Okay, now your turn.[the rule slide's images hold; closing screen: bullets — • A solid keeps shape and volume • A liquid keeps volume, not shape • A gas keeps neither]

Juice is poured from a tall glass into a wide bowl. What does the juice keep?

  1. A the juice's shape
  2. B the juice's volume
  3. C both the juice's shape and the juice's volume
Answer

Key: B — the juice's volume

A liquid keeps its volume but takes its container's shape.
So the juice keeps its volume.

Feedback per option

  • A The juice lies flat in the bowl, so it does not keep its shape.
  • B A liquid keeps its volume but takes its container's shape.
  • C Only a solid keeps both.

Which state has particles that are far apart and fly in every direction?

  1. A solid
  2. B liquid
  3. C gas
Answer

Key: C — gas

In a gas the particles are far apart with empty space between them, and they fly fast in every direction.

Feedback per option

  • A Solid particles are closely packed, in fixed positions.
  • B Liquid particles are close together and slide.
  • C Gas particles are far apart and fly.

A pan of water is heated on a stove. What happens to the speed of the water particles?

  1. A They move faster.
  2. B They move more slowly.
  3. C The particles' speed does not change.
Answer

Key: A — They move faster.

Heating gives particles energy and speeds them up.

Feedback per option

  • A Heating gives particles energy and speeds them up.
  • B Cooling slows particles down; heating speeds them up.
  • C Heating changes the particles' speed, not their size.
