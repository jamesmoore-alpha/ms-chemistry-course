Physical change: the same substance in a new form

MAT-062 · Physical change — Identify which changes are physical based on whether any new substance is formed.

Script
SayScreen
A student tears a sheet of paper into two pieces.[a sheet of paper tears in two]
She lets an ice cube melt in a glass.[an ice cube in a glass melts to liquid]
And she stirs a spoon of sugar into water until the grains vanish.[a spoon stirs; the grains vanish]
In each case, is the substance you end with the same substance you started with?[the three scenes in a row; question mark]
Yes, it is, every time.[three ticks appear faintly]
Each torn piece is still paper: zoom in, and the same paper particles are there.[paper_torn: the sheet, the pieces, the two zoom boxes]
Look at the glass: the liquid is water, the same water particles, now rearranged.[ice_to_water: the before box, the after box]
She sips the sugar water: it tastes sweet, so the sugar is still there, spread among the water particles.[sugar_into_water: the two boxes, then the mixture box]
She bends a copper wire into a hook.[a wire bends into a hook]
It is still copper.[the table row: yes]
But if an iron nail stays in the rain for a month, orange flakes grow that are not iron.[the nail row: no]
A change in which no new substance forms is called a physical change.[chip: PHYSICAL CHANGE]
So, here's what we've seen. A physical change is a change in which no new substance forms.[the rule slide: the cases table with its ticks and one cross; chip PHYSICAL CHANGE; nothing else printed]
The size, the shape or the state changes, but the particles are the same particles, arranged or moving differently.[the three after boxes side by side]
So the torn paper, the melted ice and the dissolved sugar are three physical changes.[the three scenes, each ticked under 'the same substance?']
Okay, now your turn.[closing screen: one line — “A physical change is a change in which no new substance forms.”]

“Okay, now your turn.” — the attached question

video

A sheet of paper is torn in two. What is in each piece?

  1. A the same paper particles
  2. B new particles that are not paper
  3. C no particles at all
Answer

Key: A — the same paper particles

Tearing changes the size and the shape.
Each piece is still paper.
So each piece holds the same paper particles.

Feedback per option

  • A Each piece is still paper: the same paper particles are there.
  • B Tearing makes pieces; it makes no new particle.
  • C Every piece of paper is made of paper particles.

Q1recall

A popsicle left on a plate turns into a puddle of liquid. Which change of state is this?

  1. A freezing
  2. B melting
  3. C evaporating
Answer

Key: B — melting

The popsicle was a solid.
It became a liquid.
A solid turning into a liquid is called melting.

Feedback per option

  • A Freezing is a liquid turning into a solid; here a solid turned into a liquid.
  • B A solid turning into a liquid is melting.
  • C Evaporating is a liquid turning into a gas; here a solid turned into a liquid.
Q2recall

Salt is stirred into water until no grains are left. The water is then left to evaporate. What is left in the dish?

  1. A nothing at all
  2. B a new white powder that is not salt
  3. C the salt
Answer

Key: C — the salt

Dissolved salt is salt particles spread among the water particles.
Evaporating turns the water into a gas that leaves the dish.
The salt particles stay behind, so the salt is left in the dish.

Feedback per option

  • A The water left as a gas, but the salt particles cannot; they stay in the dish.
  • B Dissolving made no new substance; the powder left behind is the salt.
  • C The salt particles were spread through the water; when the water leaves as a gas, the salt is left behind.

Chemical change: a new substance forms

MAT-063 · Chemical change — Identify which changes are chemical based on whether a new substance with different properties is formed.

Script
SayScreen
An iron nail left out for a month turns orange-brown and flaky.[a shiny nail; a month passes; orange flakes grow]
Iron is shiny gray, bends without breaking, and a magnet pulls it hard.[the shiny nail bends; a magnet grabs it]
The flakes are dull, crumble in your fingers, and a magnet barely pulls them.[flakes crumble; the magnet barely moves them]
They are not iron in a new shape. What are they?[question mark over the flakes]
They are a new substance, and the table shows it: every property differs.[iron_rust_table fills row by row]
Iron atoms in the nail joined with oxygen atoms from the air.[iron_to_rust: the iron box, the oxygen box]
The joined atoms make particles of a new substance, called rust.[the rust box appears below; chip: RUST]
Rust is not iron with oxygen stuck on top: the atoms joined into new particles.[stuck_on_vs_joined, both boxes under the question A NEW SUBSTANCE?]
A change in which a new substance forms is called a chemical change.[chip: CHEMICAL CHANGE]
Ask the same question of every change: did a new substance form?[l02_cases_table, empty last column]
Wood burning to ash, baking soda fizzing with a new gas, an egg frying solid: each gets a yes.[the three yes rows fill together]
Ice melting to water, salt dissolving: each gets a no.[the two no rows fill together]
So, a chemical change is a change in which a new substance forms.[the rule slide: the rust box; chip CHEMICAL CHANGE; nothing else printed]
The new substance has its own properties, different from the substance you started with.[iron_rust_table beside the rust box]
So the rusting nail is a chemical change.[the nail, the rust box, chip: CHEMICAL CHANGE]
Okay, now your turn.[closing screen: one line — “A chemical change is a change in which a new substance forms.”]

“Okay, now your turn.” — the attached question

video

An iron nail rusts. What do the iron atoms do?

  1. A They stay iron, with oxygen sitting on top of them.
  2. B They melt into orange flakes.
  3. C They join with oxygen atoms and make rust.
Answer

Key: C — They join with oxygen atoms and make rust.

The air holds oxygen.
Iron atoms in the nail join with oxygen atoms.
The joined atoms make particles of a new substance, rust.

Feedback per option

  • A If oxygen only sat among the iron atoms, the iron would still be iron.
  • B Nothing melts; the atoms join into new particles.
  • C Iron atoms join with oxygen atoms: particles of a new substance, rust.

The signs of a chemical change

MAT-064 · Name the signs of chemical change — Name the observable signs that a chemical change may have occurred.

Script
SayScreen
A spoon of baking soda goes into a beaker of vinegar, and it fizzes.[a beaker of vinegar; the spoon tips in; it fizzes]
A match burns with a yellow flame and warms your fingers.[a match flares]
A cut apple turns brown.[an apple browns]
Two clear liquids poured together turn cloudy white.[two liquids go cloudy]
What do these scenes have in common?[the four scenes; question mark]
In each, a chemical change happened, and something you could notice changed.[the four scenes ringed]
Nobody can see the atoms join, yet a cook, a mechanic and a doctor spot chemical changes every day.[a magnifier over the beaker finds no atoms to watch; three people at work]
The apple turned brown: a color change.[icon: color; label A COLOR CHANGE]
Bubbles poured off the vinegar: a gas forming.[icon: gas; label A GAS FORMING]
A white solid appeared where two liquids met: a solid forming, called a precipitate.[icon: solid; label A SOLID FORMING; chip: PRECIPITATE]
The match warmed your fingers: a temperature change.[icon: temperature; label A TEMPERATURE CHANGE]
And the match burned with a flame: light given out.[icon: light; label LIGHT GIVEN OUT]
Boiling water bubbles too, and boiling is a physical change.[a pan boils; label A CLUE, NOT PROOF]
So, five signs tell you a chemical change may have happened: a color change, a gas forming, a solid forming from two liquids, a temperature change, and light given out.[the rule slide: five_signs, the five panels in a row; nothing else printed]
Each sign is a clue, not proof: each can appear without a chemical change.[the boiling pan beside the five panels]
So the cook, the mechanic and the doctor watch for these five signs, then check for a new substance.[the three people; the five icons]
Okay, now your turn.[the five labeled panels hold; no closing line and no bullets: the five panel labels are the summary]

  1. A Can the change be undone afterwards?
  2. B Did a new substance form?
  3. C Did bubbles appear?
Answer

Key: B — Did a new substance form?

The signs are clues, and whether a change can be undone is not the test.
One question decides: did a new substance form?

Feedback per option

  • A Whether a change can be undone is not the test.
  • B The one test is whether a new substance formed.
  • C Bubbles are a clue, not proof; the test is whether a new substance formed.
Q2recall

Two clear liquids meet, the mixture goes cloudy, and grains settle. Which sign is this?

  1. A a gas forming, seen as bubbles or fizz
  2. B a color change to a new color
  3. C a solid forming
Answer

Key: C — a solid forming

The telling words are cloudy and grains settle.
A solid appearing where two liquids met means a precipitate formed.
So the sign is a solid forming.

Feedback per option

  • A Grains that settle are a solid, not a gas.
  • B Cloudy with grains settling is a solid forming, not only a new color.
  • C A solid appearing where two liquids met is a solid forming: a precipitate.

  1. A The runny batter turned into a brown, spongy solid.
  2. B The oven was set to a high temperature for the whole bake.
  3. C The cake was baked for exactly 30 minutes.
Answer

Key: A — The runny batter turned into a brown, spongy solid.

The evidence is what you observed about the substance.
The batter was pale and runny; it became brown, spongy and solid.
That change in the substance is the observation to cite.

Feedback per option

  • A What the batter became is the observation that tells you about a new substance.
  • B The oven setting is what was done, not what the batter became.
  • C The time is what was done, not what the batter became.
