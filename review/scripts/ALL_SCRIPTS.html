MAT-082 · Classify a substance using an indicator or pH reading — Classify a substance as acid, base, or neutral using either a litmus result or a pH value.

Suppose you have six unlabeled liquids and one clue for each.

Three come with a litmus result, three with a pH reading: sort all six into acid, base or neutral.

If the clue is litmus, read the color change. Blue turning red means acid, red turning blue means base, neither changing means neutral.

If the clue is a pH, read the scale: below 7 acid, exactly 7 neutral, above 7 base.

Now let's sort: liquid P turned blue litmus paper red, so P is an acid.

Liquid Q reads pH 9, above 7, so Q is a base.

Liquid R changed neither paper and liquid S reads pH 7, so both are neutral.

Liquid T turned red litmus paper blue, so T is a base. Liquid U reads pH 3, so U is an acid.

So, a litmus color or a pH puts a liquid in one of three boxes: acid, base or neutral.

So P and U are acids, Q and T are bases, and R and S are neutral.

Add a base to an acid: the pH climbs

MAT-081 · Neutralisation — Predict how the pH of an acidic solution changes when a base is added to it.

Suppose stomach acid at pH 2 gives you heartburn. You take two antacid tablets.

The tablets are a base, and the burning stops. What did the base do to the pH?

It made the pH climb.

Stomach acid is dilute hydrochloric acid, and the tablet's magnesium hydroxide is a base.

Magnesium hydroxide and hydrochloric acid react to make magnesium chloride and water.

The reaction uses up some of the acid, so the liquid is less acidic.

A less acidic liquid has a higher pH, so watch the marker climb toward 7.

Exactly enough base uses up every bit of acid: the marker reaches 7, and the liquid is neutral.

More base than that is left over with nothing to react with: the marker climbs past 7.

An acid and a base reacting and using each other up is called neutralization.

Lab hydrochloric acid is a strong acid that burns skin, so goggles and gloves are worn.

So, when a base is added to an acid, the base uses up some of the acid, and the pH climbs toward 7.

So the tablets used up some of the stomach acid, its pH climbed, and the burning stopped.

Where the salt comes from

MAT-080 · Salt as the product of neutralisation — Explain where the salt formed in an acid–base reaction comes from.

Suppose you add sodium hydroxide, a base, to hydrochloric acid, just enough, and the mixture reads pH 7.

When the water is boiled away, white crystals are left: sodium chloride, table salt.

Neither liquid had any salt in it, so where did the salt come from?

It came from both the acid and the base.

The atoms in a new substance come from the substances that were there before.

This neutralization makes sodium chloride and water.

The acid has two parts: a hydrogen part and a chloride part.

The base has two parts: a sodium part and a hydroxide part.

Watch the parts swap partners.

The sodium part from the base joins the chloride part from the acid: sodium chloride.

The leftover parts, hydrogen from the acid and hydroxide from the base, join: water.

A substance made this way is called a salt.

So, here's what we've seen. A neutralization makes a salt and water.

The base gives the salt its metal part.

The acid gives the salt its other part.

So the crystals' sodium came from the base and their chloride from the acid, and no atom appeared or vanished.
