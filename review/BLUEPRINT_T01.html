What this topic is, in five lines

Ten lessons, one per atom after the merge in question 1: MAT-001, 002, 003, 004, 016 (merged), 016b, 017b, 018b, 019, 019b.

Each lesson is an article and, where marked, a 40–90 second video; the student picks one; the quiz follows in the app.

Three terms are coined here and nowhere else: matter (L01), particle (L02), kinetic energy (L09). "Particle" is the only name for the pieces; "atom" and "molecule" wait for Topic 7.

Every grade 3–5 idea a lesson leans on is retrieved inside the scene and asked as one recall question. Nothing is assumed.

Every scene was chosen fresh for middle school. HS Chemistry Unit 1 was consulted for its rulings only.

What counts as matter L01

A flat football sits on a balance.

A student pumps the ball up hard.

She puts it back on the same balance.

The pumped-up ball reads about 5 g heavier.

Nothing went into the ball except air.

So the air has mass.

Is air real matter, like the ball itself?

The point, stated first

What makes something matter?

Matter is anything that has mass and takes up space.

The air has mass, because the balance climbed by about 5 g.

The air takes up space, because the pumped ball is bigger than the flat one.

So air is matter.

Light from a lamp has no mass and takes up no space, so light is not matter.

Matter is made of particles too small to see L02

A student stirs 10 g of sugar into 200 mL of water.

The sugar grains shrink and vanish.

The water looks clear.

She sips the water.

The water tastes sweet.

The sugar is gone from sight, yet the sugar is still there.

Where is it?

The point, stated first

Where did the sugar go?

The sugar broke into pieces far too small to see.

Those pieces spread through the water.

Every kind of matter is made of pieces like these.

Scientists call the pieces particles.

A sugar grain you can see is billions of particles packed together.

Particles never stop moving L03

A teacher opens a bottle of perfume at the front of a quiet classroom.

Every window is shut.

No fan runs.

Twenty seconds later the students in the back row can smell the perfume.

Nobody carried the smell to them.

How did the perfume reach the back row?

The point, stated first

How does a smell cross a still room?

Perfume particles leave the open bottle.

Particles are always moving, even in still air.

The perfume particles bump into air particles and keep moving in every direction.

They spread through the room until they reach the back row.

Heating speeds particles up; cooling slows them down L04

Two mugs each hold 250 mL of water.

One mug is at 10 °C.

The other is at 80 °C.

A student drops a tea bag into each mug and does not stir.

After 30 seconds the hot water has turned brown all the way through.

The cold water shows only a faint brown cloud around its bag.

The tea and the water are the same in both mugs.

What did the heat change?

The point, stated first

What does heating do to particles?

Heating a substance makes its particles move faster.

Cooling makes them move more slowly.

The water particles at 80 °C move faster than the water particles at 10 °C.

So they carry the tea color through the whole mug sooner.

The particles do not grow or shrink.

Only their speed changes.

Video: the color spreading at two speeds side by side — video and article.

Solid, liquid or gas? L05

A student pours dry sand from a bucket into a jar.

The sand flows and settles.

It takes the shape of the jar, just as water would.

So is sand a liquid?

The point, stated first

How do you tell a solid from a liquid from a gas?

Ask two questions: does it keep its own shape, and does it keep its own volume?

A solid keeps both.

A liquid keeps its volume but takes the shape of its container.

A gas keeps neither: it spreads to fill whatever space it is given.

One grain of sand keeps its shape and its volume, so sand is a solid.

A jar of sand is thousands of tiny solids.

Video: nothing a page cannot show — article only. The two-question test applied to six cases is a table job.

Why a solid keeps its shape L06

A steel spoon lies in a cup, then in a bowl, then in a jug.

It stays spoon-shaped in every one.

Water poured into the same three containers takes three different shapes.

What holds the spoon in one shape?

The point, stated first

Why does a solid keep its shape?

In a solid the particles are packed tightly, touching, in fixed positions.

Each particle vibrates on the spot.

No particle can move past its neighbors.

So the whole solid holds one shape and one volume.

Video: the particles vibrating in place — video and article.

Why a liquid takes the shape of its container L07

The student pours the 250 mL of juice from the tall glass into the wide bowl.

The juice flows and flattens.

The spoon did not flow.

Why can the juice change its shape while its volume stays 250 mL?

The point, stated first

Why does a liquid flow?

In a liquid the particles still touch, but they have no fixed positions.

The particles slide past one another.

Sliding particles rearrange to fit the container, so the liquid takes its shape.

The particles stay touching, so the liquid keeps its volume.

Why a gas fills any container L08

The 20 mL of air in the syringe spread out to fill 60 mL.

Then it squeezed into 10 mL.

Where did the air go when the space grew?

How did the same air fit into less?

The point, stated first

Why does a gas fill any space?

In a gas the particles are far apart, with empty space between them.

The particles fly fast in every direction.

Nothing holds them together, so they keep going until they hit the walls.

Pull the plunger and the same particles spread into the bigger space.

Push it and they crowd into the smaller space.

The particles themselves stay the same size.

Rank the states by kinetic energy L09

Three particle boxes sit side by side: ice, water and steam.

All three hold the same water particles.

In one box the particles vibrate on the spot.

In one they slide.

In one they fly.

Which particles carry the most energy of movement?

The point, stated first

Which state has the fastest particles?

Energy of movement is called kinetic energy.

Gas particles fly fastest, so they have the most kinetic energy.

Liquid particles slide, so they have less.

Solid particles only vibrate, so they have the least.

Solid, liquid, gas: least to most.

Why gas particles have the most kinetic energy L10

A pan holds water at 20 °C.

The burner heats it.

At 100 °C steam rises off the pan.

In the water the particles slid past one another.

In the steam they fly.

Where did the extra energy of movement come from?

The point, stated first

Why do gas particles have the most kinetic energy?

The faster a particle moves, the more kinetic energy it has.

Heating gives particles energy and speeds them up.

A gas has taken in more energy than the same substance as a liquid or a solid.

So its particles move fastest and carry the most kinetic energy.

  • Fourteen topics, one PP100 at the end of each, 92 lessons in all. One atom, one lesson. Topics run 4 to 12 atoms; the average is 6 or 7.
  • The order follows the big ideas, not the TEKS list: what matter is and how particles explain it → what properties tell you → sorting matter into kinds → inside the atom and the periodic table → how matter changes → two grade-8 applications (water, acids and bases).

It stresses every treatment the course will need — a two-part test taught by examples, a fact, two "explain the smell" mechanisms, three example-sequence classifications, three particle-picture why-lessons, one coined term with a ranking routine. Card 2 — Topic 1 is the pilot, at 12 lessons

The call: pilot "Particles and the three states of matter" end to end — 12 lessons, quiz after each, mixed practice, PP100 — before any other topic starts.

Why
